The big picture: the AI trade cracked abroad, and India became a place to hide

What changed, how the pieces connect, and the one map to read first.

In short
  • Big investors sold AI and chip shares hard in Korea, Taiwan and the US.
  • Some of that money came to India, so the Nifty had its best day in 4 months.
  • But Indian prices, the rupee and smaller shares are still under pressure.

For most of this year, Indian shares lagged because world money chased AI companies in the US, Korea and Taiwan. That trade cracked. Korea's main index fell 8% in a day, chip shares had their longest losing run in 16 years, and lenders started to doubt the companies that rent out AI computers.

Why money moved

  • AI doubts — Anthropic's quarterly sales grew 45%, slower than the jump some had expected, and AI bond prices fell (section 03).
  • Hot US prices — US producer prices rose 1.3% in one month, so the Fed may raise rates again (section 02).
  • Bonds as shelter — the US 10-year yield (the return lenders demand for 10 years) fell back towards 5% as money ran to safety.
  • India's cheapness — after a year of lagging, Indian shares looked cheap next to Korea and Taiwan, which had risen far more.

One view is that this is the start of a long turn: India did badly when AI was rising, so it should do well when AI falls. The opposite view is that it was one day of short-covering (traders buying back shares they had bet against), and foreigners still sold ₹5,285 crore of Indian shares in cash on that day.

What still hurts

  • Prices — retail inflation is 5.54% and wholesale inflation 11.39%, mostly fuel.
  • Rupee — near 95 to the dollar, with the RBI selling dollars to hold it.
  • Oil — Brent is near $105, with Iran attacking ships near Hormuz.
  • Small shares — the smallcap index fell 1.3% on the Nifty's best day.

Reading between the lines, the AI boom and India's rate pain are linked. AI companies borrowed huge sums in the US bond market, which pushed US yields up and pulled money out of India. When AI borrowing stopped, US yields fell and India got relief. So the fall of AI lending is, for now, good news for Indian borrowers.

02

The price of money: hot prices in the US, a tight RBI at home

US inflation, the Fed, Indian inflation, the RBI's rate rise and the rupee.

In short
  • US prices rose faster than expected, mostly because of fuel.
  • India's prices are rising too, so the RBI raised its rate for the first time since 2023.
  • Rates are unlikely to fall soon, in either country.

US prices ran hot

US consumer prices rose 3.7% in a year to September, and producer prices (what businesses charge each other) rose 6.3%. The monthly jump in producer prices was twice the forecast, and most of the rise in goods prices came from energy.

  • Petrol — up 34.2% in a year and more than half of September's rise in consumer prices.
  • Wholesale petrol — up 20.3% in one month; energy up 10.8%.
  • Shoppers — retail sales rose 0.8%, but the "control group" (sales that feed into GDP) rose only 0.1%, so real spending likely fell.
  • Jobs — weekly jobless claims were 199,000, close to the lowest in 57 years.

Views split on what the Fed does next. One view, from Fed official Mary Daly, is that more rises are "probably needed" but not urgent. Deutsche Bank sees a growing risk of a 0.50% rise in one go. The opposite view is that the Fed is boxed in by government debt and will let inflation run, which is why some argue for gold.

Bonds rallied anyway

Normally hot prices push bond yields up. This time the US 10-year yield fell to about 5.08%, from a spike near 5.33%, because money fled chip shares into government bonds. Shorter yields fell even faster.

Reading between the lines, the bond rally is fear, not relief about inflation. A yield that falls because stocks are crashing can rise again quickly once the panic passes. Goldman Sachs chief David Solomon said a 5% 10-year rate is normal and zero rates were the oddity.

India: the RBI turned

The RBI raised its repo rate (what it charges banks for short loans) by 0.25% to 5.50% on 7 October. It was the first rise since February 2023, and the RBI moved its stance from "neutral" to "calibrated tightening", which signals more may come.

Why chip shares and AI lenders fell, and why the chip makers themselves still look strong.

In short

The lenders said no

CoreWeave rents out AI computers and borrows heavily to buy them. Its 2032 bond, sold at a 9.625% rate in June, now yields about 16%. The price of insuring its debt now implies a high chance of default.

Big AI-linked bond deals in the US ($3bn and above), $bn a month

A Bloomberg timeline shared in the material: 23 deals worth $378.2bn, none since August.

The chart is the key fact of the week. Big AI companies sold $378bn of high-grade bonds in 23 deals, then nothing in September or October. Goldman Sachs put it plainly: demand for computing can grow very fast, but the money to pay for it cannot.

  • Funding gap — Deutsche Bank says CoreWeave needs about $45bn of spending but makes about $10bn of cash, a $35bn gap.
  • Weak hands — Gartner predicts over 80% of "neoclouds" (small AI-computer rental firms) will fold or be bought.
  • Junk bonds — 20 data-centre project bonds worth $45.6bn sit in the junk market; CoreWeave is the tenant on 7.
  • Support — Nvidia boss Jensen Huang said CoreWeave's cost of money is "actually decreasing"; Nvidia owns a stake of about $2bn.

Anthropic: strong numbers, high hopes

Anthropic told investors its July-September sales were $16.8bn, up 45% on the quarter before, and that it made an adjusted profit. Reuters reported that some investors now value its planned November share sale lower, near $1.5 trillion.

The makers are fine

  • Share supply — share sales by SpaceX, Anthropic and OpenAI could add 6% to 7.5% of US market value in a year; one fund manager links that size of supply to a fall of 18% to 20%.
  • Bear call — investor Paul Tudor Jones said he will bet against stocks for all of 2027.

Reading between the lines, the problem is not the chips but who pays for them. The sellers of picks and shovels (TSMC, ASML) are paid up front. The renters in the middle borrowed to buy, and now face expensive money. So the pain sits with the borrowers, and it reaches India through bond yields and fund flows, not through Indian companies.

Small shares: the pain moved down

Smaller shares had led since April. The Smallcap 250 index is still up about 10% since May while the Nifty is down about 5%. But in October, traders using borrowed money through MTF (margin trading facility, where a broker lends part of the price) sold before results, and the falls fed on themselves.

  • Valuation — one data site's price-to-earnings figure for the Smallcap 100 jumped from about 31 to 38, which looks like a change in method rather than a real rise.

  • But — 1-year smallcap returns have ranged from -69.6% to +175.4%.

SEBI steps back on expiry day

SEBI will go back to settling expiring derivatives on the average price of the last half hour of trade, from 7 December. The closing auction session (a short window after the close that sets the day's closing price) stays for the cash market. Exchange and broker shares rose on the news.

  • Timing — expiring index contracts stop at 3:30 pm; stock futures trade till 3:45 pm.
  • SME share sales — SEBI proposes using market value, not paid-up capital, to decide where a company lists; up to ₹1,000 crore goes to the SME platform.
  • SME profit test — an average operating profit of ₹3 crore over 3 years, up from ₹1 crore in 2 of 3 years.
  • Foreign tax — the government is studying relief for foreign investors caught by the Tiger Global ruling on treaty benefits.

Reading between the lines, the government and SEBI are reversing several moves that hurt trading volumes and foreign interest. STT (securities transaction tax) collections are up 51.6% this year, so the state earns more when traders trade, which is one reason the rules are being eased.

05

Oil, ships and war: the barrel is dear, diesel is dearer

Hormuz, diesel, reserves, Russia's oil and what it means for India's import bill.

In short
  • Oil stays above $100 because Iran keeps attacking ships near Hormuz.
  • Diesel is the tightest fuel, and governments are running out of tricks.
  • India faces a US decision on tariffs for buyers of Russian oil.

Where prices sit

Brent crude, the main world price, held near $105 a barrel, up about 73% this year. Physical cargoes for quick delivery cost even more. US crude stocks fell by 8.5 million barrels in a week when a small rise was expected, the biggest drop since the war began.

One view is that "paper" oil (futures) looks calm while real barrels are short, so prices must rise further and the official claim that Hormuz flows are back to normal is wrong. The opposite view, held by the IEA (the rich countries' energy agency), is that reserve releases will cover the gap, and it still forecasts a surplus in 2027.

Diesel is the real squeeze

Diesel runs trucks, tractors, ships and generators, so its price spreads into food and freight. The IEA says the diesel shortage could last into the first half of 2027.

  • Europe — a German airport began running out of jet fuel; pilots were told to fill up elsewhere.
  • US farms — Michigan declared an energy emergency and eased diesel rules for farmers.
  • Reserves — IEA members have released about 330 million barrels and will pass the 400 million promised in March, including over 20 million barrels of diesel.
  • US reserve — the US did not draw its own emergency stock in the latest week, for the first time since the war began.
  • Ships — the US is weighing easing sanctions on "shadow fleet" tankers to cut record freight costs.

Reading between the lines, each fix moves the problem. Easing tanker sanctions lowers freight costs but puts more Russian and Iranian oil on the water, which helps buyers like India but weakens pressure on Iran. Releasing reserves lowers prices now but leaves less cushion for later.

The war around the Gulf

  • Hormuz — Iran's army chief said the strait stays closed until Iran's "rights" are respected; another tanker caught fire east of the strait.
  • US plans — CNN reported US strike plans that could use the "mother of all bombs" (a huge air-burst bomb) on Iranian energy and nuclear sites; President Trump said he would not use a nuclear bomb.
  • Saudi Arabia — the Houthis struck Saudi airports and energy sites; France sent troops and radars to guard the Yanbu oil port on the Red Sea.
  • Ukraine — Kyiv said it will resume drone strikes on Russian refineries; a large attack hit the Ryazan refinery.
  • Cost — Moody's top economist estimates the Iran war has cost each US household nearly $2,000.

India's Russian oil question

Fertiliser is made from natural gas, and the Gulf is a big supplier. One forecast argues that farmers using less fertiliser now will grow less next season, so food prices rise in 2027, with India hit first among large importers. The US cattle herd is already the smallest since 1951.

06

India's economy: busy factories, strong exports, cautious shoppers

Trade, tax, the balance of payments, festive spending and costs for businesses.

In short
  • Exports grew fast and the trade gap narrowed.
  • Tax, credit and GST all grew well, so the economy is busy.
  • But shoppers are spending less per order, and phones cost much more.

Exports had a big month

Goods exports rose 23% in September, the 6th straight month of double-digit growth. Imports grew far less, because gold imports collapsed. So the goods trade gap narrowed from a year ago.

  • Exports — $44.4bn in September.
  • Imports — $74.6bn, up 8%, a 6-month low; gold imports fell 81%.
  • Goods gap — $30.2bn, against $33bn a year ago.
  • First half — exports of $259.9bn, up 18.5%.

The money coming in

  • Balance of payments — August showed a $45.4bn surplus, helped by a $50.6bn capital inflow; non-resident deposits reached $83bn.
  • Current account gap — narrowed to $5.2bn in August from $6.1bn a year earlier.

  • Trade deals — the India-New Zealand deal starts 20 October; India-EU signing is set for 16 December; Canada aims for December.

One view is that the FDI gap shows foreign owners selling Indian stakes, often private equity buying and selling hospitals and banks, which is not new factories. The opposite view is that letting investors take money out is what makes them willing to bring more in.

A preferential issue is a sale of new shares or warrants to chosen buyers, often promoters or funds, instead of the public.
